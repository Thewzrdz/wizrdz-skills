# Federal 2210 IT Series — Application & STAR Interview Runbook

A field-tested guide to navigating the GS-2210 IT Management (Information
Technology Management) series application process: from resume to HR referral,
through structured competency-based panel interviews. Built from real experience
applying on USAJobs and going through the HR screen and STAR interview process.

The GS-2210 series covers IT Specialist roles across specialties: Security,
Systems Administration, Applications Software, Customer Support, Network Services,
Data Management, Internet, Policy and Planning, and Systems Analysis. This runbook
focuses on Security and Systems Administration specialties, which are the highest-
demand and most competitive.

---

## Phase 1 — Resume and self-assessment (before you apply)

### 1a. Understand the federal resume format
Federal resumes are NOT the same as private-sector resumes. They are long (3–5
pages is normal), highly detailed, and read by HR specialists who are matching
your text against a vacancy announcement, not a recruiter skimming for names.

Requirements for a federal resume that doesn't get auto-filtered:
- **Full dates:** month/year to month/year for every position, plus hours per week
- **Salary or pay rate** for each prior position (optional but expected)
- **Employer contact info** (supervisor name, phone, may-we-contact)
- **Detailed duties:** write out what you actually did, including tools, scale,
  and any quantifiable impact — HR is checking specific keywords
- **Education:** include GPA if strong; list relevant coursework for STEM/Cyber
  roles; include in-progress degrees with expected completion

### 1b. Map your experience to the 2210 specialty area competencies
Each 2210 vacancy lists a set of competencies. Common Security specialty competencies:
- Information Security
- Infrastructure Design / System Administration
- Risk Management (NIST RMF, FISMA)
- Incident Response
- Network and Internet Security
- Oral Communication / Written Communication
- Problem Solving

For each competency: identify a concrete story from your work history that
demonstrates it. Write one sentence per story before the interview. These become
your STAR answers.

**Your certifications and how they map:**
- **CompTIA Security+ (SY0-701):** covers Information Security, Network Security,
  Risk Management — cite it in your resume header and in any answer touching
  security fundamentals
- **CySA+ (in progress):** covers threat detection, analysis, incident response —
  reference it even in-progress; list expected completion date
- **WGU B.S. Cybersecurity (in progress):** maps directly to the education
  requirement for GS-7/9/11 positions; include expected graduation
- **SDSU Cybersecurity Certificate:** supplemental credential, add to education section
- **FEMA ICS-100:** relevant for any position involving government continuity ops

### 1c. Know which GS grade you're targeting
- **GS-7:** one year of specialized experience at GS-5 equivalent, OR a full year
  of graduate study, OR superior academic achievement
- **GS-9:** one year at GS-7 equivalent, OR a master's degree
- **GS-11:** one year at GS-9 equivalent — typically where 2210 Security roles
  start for candidates with certs + relevant work experience
- **GS-12+:** requires specific years of progressively responsible experience;
  certifications alone won't bridge the gap

Realistic target with Security+ + CySA+ in progress + relevant IT experience +
active degree program: **GS-9 or GS-11** depending on the specific agency and
announcement language.

## Phase 2 — The USAJobs application

### 2a. Read the announcement — all of it
The Qualifications section contains the exact words HR will use to screen you.
Your resume must use the same terminology (not synonyms) for the required
specialized experience. HR is not inferring; they are matching text.

Key sections to read carefully:
- **Who May Apply** — some announcements are status-only (current federal employees)
  or open to all US citizens; confirm you're eligible before spending time
- **Specialized Experience** — copy this text. Write a bullet in your resume for
  every requirement listed, using the same vocabulary where possible.
- **Education substitution** — many GS-9/11 announcements allow a relevant master's
  degree to substitute for experience; WGU degree + Security+ often qualifies
- **Assessment questions** — these are the 1-10 self-ratings; answer honestly but
  confidently; inflating scores only hurts if you can't back them up at interview

### 2b. The resume builder vs. uploading your own
USAJobs Resume Builder: fill it out if the announcement requires it (some agencies
mandate this). It's clunky but it forces you to include all required fields (hours,
supervisor, may-we-contact) that HR checks. If the announcement allows uploading
your own PDF/Word doc, that format is fine — but it must still contain all those
fields.

### 2c. Assessment questionnaire strategy
Most 2210 announcements use Questionnaire + Resume screening before interview.
The questionnaire self-scores your competencies 1–5 (never done this to expert).
Do not rate yourself 1–2 on anything genuinely relevant to your background. Do
not rate yourself 5 unless you can give a strong concrete story for it.
Realistic calibration with Security+ + 2–3 years IT experience:
- Security fundamentals: 4
- Incident response: 3–4 depending on hands-on depth
- Risk management / FISMA: 3 (unless you have FedRAMP or RMF experience)
- Network security: 3–4

## Phase 3 — HR screen and referral

### 3a. What happens after you apply
1. HR reviews résumé against minimum qualifications (automated + manual)
2. Assessment questionnaire scores generate a composite score
3. If you're in the referred pool, hiring manager receives your package
4. Hiring manager selects candidates for interview

**The referral is the gate.** Getting referred means your résumé cleared HR;
not getting referred usually means a keyword miss on specialized experience.
If you're not getting referred: compare your résumé line-by-line to the
specialized experience text in the announcement and add the missing terms.

### 3b. Reasonable timeline
- Application open: 1–2 weeks typically
- HR review and referral: 2–8 weeks (varies widely by agency)
- Interview scheduling: 1–4 weeks after referral
- Selection and offer: 2–6 weeks after interview
- Onboarding (clearance, EOD scheduling): 4–12 weeks depending on investigation level

Total applicant-to-start: **3–6 months is normal.** Apply early; maintain other
pipelines simultaneously.

## Phase 4 — The STAR panel interview

Federal interviews are structured: every candidate gets the same questions, every
answer is scored against the same rubric, and the panel writes notes during your
response. The goal is consistency and legal defensibility, not conversational flow.

### 4a. STAR format
Every answer follows: Situation → Task → Action → Result

- **Situation (10–15%):** Brief context. One or two sentences. Where, when, what
  role, what was the problem.
- **Task (10–15%):** What were YOU specifically responsible for solving. "We" is
  a red flag — interviewers are scoring your individual contribution.
- **Action (60–70%):** The bulk of your answer. What you did step-by-step.
  Be specific: what tool, what decision, what constraint, what tradeoff. This is
  where you demonstrate competency — not just that you did something but HOW.
- **Result (15%):** Quantified if possible. Resolved the incident, reduced false
  positives by X%, cut remediation time from Y to Z, system uptime restored
  within SLA. "We closed the ticket" is weak. "The site came back clean, Google
  removed the Safe Browsing flag within 48 hours, and we added hardening that
  prevented a repeat in 6 months of monitoring" is strong.

### 4b. Preparing your STAR stories
Before the interview, write out full STAR answers for each core competency the
announcement listed. Do not memorize word-for-word; memorize the S-T-A-R skeleton.
The Action section is where you'll naturally riff from your real experience.

Target: 4–6 prepared stories that you can flex to fit different competency questions.
Most questions can be answered with 2–3 of your strongest stories adapted to angle.

### 4c. Common 2210 Security interview questions
Prepare for these categories (exact wording varies):
- "Tell me about a time you identified and responded to a security incident."
- "Describe a time you had to explain a security risk to a non-technical audience."
- "Tell me about a situation where you had to prioritize multiple security issues
  simultaneously. How did you decide what to work on first?"
- "Describe your experience with vulnerability management. Walk me through your
  process."
- "Tell me about a time a project or remediation didn't go as planned. What
  happened and what did you do?"
- "Describe a time you had to learn a new tool or technology quickly to solve
  a problem."

### 4d. What NOT to do in a federal panel interview
- Don't say "we" without immediately following with "my specific role was..."
- Don't give hypothetical answers ("I would...") — every question is behavioral,
  asking what you DID, not what you would do
- Don't rush. Panels take notes; a brief pause while you structure your answer
  reads as composure, not hesitation
- Don't oversell: if you say "expert level" anywhere, expect a follow-up that
  probes that claim at depth
- Don't forget to tie the result back to impact — the result sentence is what
  closes the scoring rubric for most panels

## Phase 5 — After the interview

- Send a brief thank-you email to the HR POC (not the panel directly unless they
  gave you their contact) within 24 hours. One short paragraph. Professional, not
  effusive.
- Timeline from interview to offer: 2–8 weeks. Ask HR for the estimated decision
  timeline at the end of the interview.
- If not selected: you can request feedback. Not all agencies provide it, but asking
  signals professionalism and you occasionally get useful specifics.
- Keep applying to other announcements in parallel. Federal hiring timelines are
  long enough that you can have multiple concurrent applications without conflict.

## Guardrails

- This skill describes application and interview process strategy only. It does
  not guarantee selection, referral, or any specific hiring outcome.
- All STAR stories must be accurate representations of your real experience.
  Fabricating or materially inflating your experience in a federal application is
  a serious matter; federal employment fraud has legal consequences.
- Specific qualification requirements vary by agency and announcement. Always read
  the full vacancy announcement and contact the HR POC listed if you have
  questions about whether your experience qualifies.
- Security clearance requirements (if any) are separate from the application
  process described here. Don't assume a clearance you don't currently hold will
  be granted.
